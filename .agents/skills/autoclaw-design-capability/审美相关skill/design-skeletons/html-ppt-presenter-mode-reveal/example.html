// part 02 · presenter view

Presenter view should have four panels

①
Current slide (large)

Takes up over half the view, so you can glance and know exactly what the audience sees.

②
Next slide preview

Helps you prepare transition lines, avoiding "what was I supposed to say on the next slide?"

③
Speaker notes area

Large font, high contrast, scrollable — this is what you're actually reading while presenting.

④
Timer + page counter

Know how long you've been talking, how many slides remain. Rhythm depends on this.

Presenter mode should give you four panels. I'll rank them by importance.

First, current slide (large). This has to take up over half the view because it's your "sync anchor" with the audience — what they see, you need to see too.

Second, next slide preview. A lot of people don't understand why this needs to be there. Here's why: the most awkward moment in a talk isn't when you're on a slide — it's the 2 seconds when you transition to the next one. If you can see the next slide ahead of time, the transition line comes naturally.

Third, speaker notes area — this is the main topic today, and I'll go deep in the next section. But one hard requirement: font size must be large, contrast must be high, and it must scroll. Because when you're presenting, you only have peripheral vision to glance at it — small text is unreadable in that moment.

Fourth, timer and page counter. Knowing how long you've been talking and how many slides remain — rhythm depends entirely on this. Keynote does it best, reveal.js isn't clear enough by default.

All four are essential. I've built all four into today's deck — press S to try it yourself.

// part 03 · script

3 iron rules for speaker notes

01
Not a verbatim script — it's a "cue signal"

Bold the key points, isolate transition sentences, list data and names clearly — so you can glance and pick right up.

02
150–300 words per slide, no more, no less

Under 150 words = insufficient cues. Over 300 words = you can't scan it fast enough. Aim for 2–3 minutes per slide.

03
Write in spoken language, not formal prose

"Therefore" → "So"; "The aforementioned solution" → "This approach". Read it aloud — it should sound like someone talking.

Now the most critical section — how to write speaker notes. I've distilled it into 3 iron rules.

Rule one: speaker notes are not a script. A lot of people hear "speaker notes" and think they need to write down every single word they'll say. Wrong. If you read from a script, the audience will notice immediately, and your credibility collapses.

The real function of speaker notes is "cue signals" — bold the key takeaways, isolate transition sentences, list specific data and proper names. This way, when you're presenting, you glance and can pick right up, but what you actually say is still your own words.

Rule two: keep it 150–300 words per slide. This is an empirical number I've arrived at after doing dozens of talks. Under 150 = not enough cues, you'll stall mid-slide. Over 300 = you can't scan it in the time you have. At 2–3 minutes per slide, this range is just right.

Rule three: write in spoken language. This is where most people trip up. You write "Therefore," but when you say it, it comes out "So." You write "The aforementioned solution," but you'll say "This approach." Read your notes aloud while writing — if it sounds awkward, rewrite it.

Combine these three rules, and you'll find that presenting slides suddenly becomes comfortable.
